# Brand Strategy

## The core insight

Every heritage house sells the same three things in different proportions: **lineage** (founding date, dynastic control), **a signature motif** (something instantly recognizable), and **control over the object's origin story**. New entrants that lack lineage substitute **transparency and founder authenticity** instead.

This business can do something none of the reference brands can: combine Graff-style vertical control (mine to masterpiece) with **genuine, lab-verified geographic origin** for colored stones. Cartier, Bulgari, Tiffany, and Van Cleef & Arpels buy colored stones on the open market — they cannot make an origin claim as credible as a Sri Lankan house that owns the relationship with Ratnapura miners and in-house cutters. Ceylon-origin sapphires already carry a documented 20–50%+ market premium over undisclosed-origin stones when backed by a GIA/Gübelin/SSEF origin report. That premium is currently captured by gem dealers and auction houses — a vertically integrated brand can capture it directly and make it the center of the brand story, the way De Beers built Forevermark around "fewer than 1% of diamonds qualify."

## Brand pillars (recommended)

1. **Provenance you can verify, not just claim** — every signature piece ships with a lab origin report and a house-issued provenance mark (serial + engraving, see Phase 3 in the [roadmap](04-roadmap.md) for the NFC/digital passport layer).
2. **The maker is named** — unlike stones bought anonymously on the open market, the cutter and setter can be credited by name or atelier, echoing the founder-authenticity move DTC brands use in place of heritage.
3. **An island, not just a supplier** — Sri Lanka (historically "Serendib" / "Ratnapura, City of Gems") is the story, not a sourcing footnote. This is the same move Chopard made with "responsibly sourced," but rooted in geography instead of ethics-only messaging (ethics can still be layered in — see below).
4. **Modern craft, old material** — gold/silver/platinum work that reads as contemporary design, not costume-heritage pastiche. Avoid over-borrowing European luxury signifiers (Belle Époque motifs, French wordmarks) — they read as imitation rather than authority when applied to a non-European house.

## Name candidates (working shortlist — verify trademark + domain availability before committing)

| Candidate | Rationale |
|---|---|
| **Ratnapura & Co.** | Names the actual "City of Gems"; follows the Tiffany/Cartier "House of / & Co." convention with real provenance baked in. |
| **Serendib House** | Serendib is the historic name for Sri Lanka (root of "serendipity") — old-world mystique that's unmistakably island-specific rather than borrowed European flavor. |
| **Cornflower & Co.** | "Cornflower blue" is the trade term for the most prized Ceylon sapphire hue — embeds a quality claim in the name itself, the way Tiffany fused a color to its house. |
| **Mayura** | Sinhala/Sanskrit for peacock, Sri Lanka's national bird — gives a signature local motif in the way Cartier has the panther and Van Cleef has the four-leaf clover. |
| **Isle & Stone** | Shorter, modern two-word name (register of "Vrai") — a good candidate for a younger sub-line or the digital-first entry brand, if a more heritage-coded name is reserved for the flagship. |

Recommendation: pick one heritage-coded name as the house (Ratnapura & Co. or Serendib House), and reserve a motif (Mayura / peacock, or a stylized lotus) as the recurring emblem across packaging, engraving, and site iconography — don't rely on the wordmark alone to carry brand recognition.

## Visual identity direction

- **Color** — a proprietary accent in the cornflower-blue or Ceylon-sapphire family, paired with black/ivory/stone neutrals. A registered "house blue" (à la Tiffany Blue) is a long-term trademark asset, not just a style choice.
- **Typography** — an engraved-capital or refined serif wordmark for the logotype, restrained sans-serif for UI/body copy. Heritage in the mark, usability in the interface.
- **Imagery** — macro product photography on dark/neutral backdrops as the default; lifestyle/editorial imagery used sparingly and never "salesy." Occasional black-and-white archival-style imagery of the Ratnapura mines/ateliers builds the provenance story visually.
- **Motif** — one emblem (peacock, lotus, or a stylized gem-cut facet) used consistently across product engraving, packaging, and digital iconography — this is what gets recognized at a glance once the wordmark isn't visible.
- **Navigation/tone** — a spare top-level structure that gives storytelling equal billing with shop categories (e.g., *High Jewellery / Bridal / Ceylon Provenance / The Maison / Boutiques*) and restrained CTAs ("Discover," "Request an Appointment") rather than "Buy Now" — matches how Graff/Bulgari/Cartier frame commerce as invitation rather than transaction.

## Pricing posture

Follow the pattern the reference brands actually use, not pure "price on request" everywhere: **catalog-priced for bridal and fine-jewellery lines** (transparent pricing correlates with higher realized prices and longer customer relationships), **POR gated behind an advisor for one-of-a-kind High Jewellery pieces**. See [docs/02-ux-design-patterns.md](02-ux-design-patterns.md) for why full opacity backfires for a brand still building trust.

## Open decisions for the client

- Final house name + motif (trademark and `.com` / relevant ccTLD availability check needed before any design work starts).
- Whether to formally pursue Responsible Jewellery Council (RJC) certification as a supporting ethical-sourcing credential alongside the geographic-provenance story.
- Whether family/founder story is used publicly (founder-authenticity is a strong substitute for centuries of heritage, per the DTC brands studied — Mejuri, Vrai, Aurate all lead with founder narrative).
