# Brand Strategy

## The core insight

Every heritage house sells the same three things in different proportions: **lineage** (founding date, dynastic control), **a signature motif** (something instantly recognizable), and **control over the object's origin story**. New entrants that lack lineage substitute **transparency and founder authenticity** instead.

This business can do something none of the reference brands can: combine Graff-style vertical control (mine to masterpiece) with **genuine, lab-verified geographic origin** for colored stones. Cartier, Bulgari, Tiffany, and Van Cleef & Arpels buy colored stones on the open market — they cannot make an origin claim as credible as a Sri Lankan house that owns the relationship with Ratnapura miners and in-house cutters. Ceylon-origin sapphires already carry a documented 20–50%+ market premium over undisclosed-origin stones when backed by a GIA/Gübelin/SSEF origin report. That premium is currently captured by gem dealers and auction houses — a vertically integrated brand can capture it directly and make it the center of the brand story, the way De Beers built Forevermark around "fewer than 1% of diamonds qualify."

## Brand pillars (recommended)

1. **Provenance you can verify, not just claim** — every signature piece ships with a lab origin report and a house-issued provenance mark (serial + engraving, see Phase 3 in the [roadmap](04-roadmap.md) for the NFC/digital passport layer).
2. **The maker is named** — unlike stones bought anonymously on the open market, the cutter and setter can be credited by name or atelier, echoing the founder-authenticity move DTC brands use in place of heritage.
3. **An island, not just a supplier** — Sri Lankan origin is the story, not a sourcing footnote. This is the same move Chopard made with "responsibly sourced," but rooted in geography instead of ethics-only messaging. Important: this lives in the *narrative and certification* (the "Our Story" pages, the origin reports, the provenance mark), not necessarily in the *name* — see naming direction below, which the client has steered toward an international register rather than a Sinhala/local-language wordmark.
4. **Modern craft, old material** — gold/silver/platinum work that reads as contemporary design, not costume-heritage pastiche. Avoid over-borrowing European luxury signifiers (Belle Époque motifs, French wordmarks) — they read as imitation rather than authority when applied to a non-European house.

## Name candidates

**Direction, per client steer:** no Sinhala/local-language or place-literal names (Ratnapura, Serendib, Mayura, Ceylonite, Vaidurya were all considered and explicitly rejected — client wants the name itself to sit in the same international/European register as Cartier, Bulgari, Chopard, Van Cleef & Arpels, and Monaco Chain, not signal "local" on first read). This is a legitimate, common split in this industry: **the origin story lives in the narrative and the certification, not necessarily in the wordmark.** De Beers didn't call its provenance line "Botswana" — it called it Forevermark. The Ceylon-provenance strategy from earlier in this doc is unaffected; it just isn't the naming mechanism.

First shortlist (Ratnapura & Co., Serendib House, Cornflower & Co., Mayura, Isle & Stone) was checked for real-world conflicts and **three of five had direct collisions**: Serendib is already used by multiple Sri Lankan gem/jewellery businesses (notably Royal Serendib Group, whose positioning — connecting Ceylon gemstone heritage to the global market — is nearly identical to this brand's own); Mayura is heavily saturated across Indian/Sri Lankan jewellery brands; Isle & Stone is an existing jewelry brand at isleandstonejewelry.com. Ratnapura is a real city name already used by several local jewellers (Ratnalaya, Ratna Jewellery) and is legally weak as a geographically-descriptive mark regardless. Cornflower & Co. had no direct jewellery-category conflict found and remains viable as a considered option, but reads more "considered gift brand" than "high jewellery house."

**Revised shortlist (international/posh register, quick-checked for conflicts):**

| Candidate | Check result | Rationale |
|---|---|---|
| **Maison Cassian** | No existing jewellery brand found under this name. | Reads as an old Parisian atelier surname — same construction as Chopard, Boucheron, Chaumet; "Maison" is the standard French luxury-house prefix (Dior, Chanel, Mellerio all use it). Strongest candidate found so far. |
| **Cassian & Vale** | Same root name; no conflict found. | Two-surname construction in the register of Van Cleef & Arpels — if a founder-duo story (real or narrative) is wanted, this reads more "house" than a single name. |
| **Maison Auvray** | No direct jewellery-brand conflict; one near-neighbor spelling exists (Maison Aubry, a real but small Cannes jewellery shop) — not a legal conflict but worth a final check. | Same French-heritage register, slightly softer sound than Cassian. |

**Rejected from the international round:** *Castellane* — collides directly with Victoire de Castellane, the globally known creative director of Dior Joaillerie for 25 years; using this name in the same industry would be actively confusing, not just crowded. *Aurelle* — already used by at least four existing jewellery brands (Dutch Aurelle, Maison Aurelle in Sherman Oaks, Aurielle Co, and others).

**Recommendation:** lead with **Maison Cassian** (or **Cassian & Vale** if a two-name "house" construction is preferred). Reserve a motif — not tied to a literal Sri Lankan symbol unless the client wants that — as the recurring emblem across packaging, engraving, and site iconography; don't rely on the wordmark alone to carry recognition.

**Still required before final commitment, for whichever name is chosen:** a formal trademark search across the relevant classes (jewellery/Class 14 at minimum) in every market the brand will sell into, run by an actual trademark attorney — the checks above are web-search diligence, not legal clearance — plus live domain availability (`.com` and the client's preferred ccTLDs).

## Visual identity direction

- **Color** — a proprietary accent in the cornflower-blue or Ceylon-sapphire family, paired with black/ivory/stone neutrals. A registered "house blue" (à la Tiffany Blue) is a long-term trademark asset, not just a style choice.
- **Typography** — an engraved-capital or refined serif wordmark for the logotype, restrained sans-serif for UI/body copy. Heritage in the mark, usability in the interface.
- **Imagery** — macro product photography on dark/neutral backdrops as the default; lifestyle/editorial imagery used sparingly and never "salesy." Occasional black-and-white archival-style imagery of the Ratnapura mines/ateliers builds the provenance story visually.
- **Motif** — one emblem used consistently across product engraving, packaging, and digital iconography — this is what gets recognized at a glance once the wordmark isn't visible. Given the naming direction above, default to an abstract or gem-form motif (e.g., a stylized facet, a monogram of the chosen name) rather than a literal Sri Lankan symbol, unless the client specifically wants to reintroduce that layer later.
- **Navigation/tone** — a spare top-level structure that gives storytelling equal billing with shop categories (e.g., *High Jewellery / Bridal / Ceylon Provenance / The Maison / Boutiques*) and restrained CTAs ("Discover," "Request an Appointment") rather than "Buy Now" — matches how Graff/Bulgari/Cartier frame commerce as invitation rather than transaction.

## Pricing posture

Follow the pattern the reference brands actually use, not pure "price on request" everywhere: **catalog-priced for bridal and fine-jewellery lines** (transparent pricing correlates with higher realized prices and longer customer relationships), **POR gated behind an advisor for one-of-a-kind High Jewellery pieces**. See [docs/02-ux-design-patterns.md](02-ux-design-patterns.md) for why full opacity backfires for a brand still building trust.

## Open decisions for the client

- Final house name + motif (trademark and `.com` / relevant ccTLD availability check needed before any design work starts).
- Whether to formally pursue Responsible Jewellery Council (RJC) certification as a supporting ethical-sourcing credential alongside the geographic-provenance story.
- Whether family/founder story is used publicly (founder-authenticity is a strong substitute for centuries of heritage, per the DTC brands studied — Mejuri, Vrai, Aurate all lead with founder narrative).
